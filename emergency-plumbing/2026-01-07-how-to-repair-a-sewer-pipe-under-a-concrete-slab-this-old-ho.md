Understanding the Problem With Repairing a Sewer Pipe

Before diving into the repair process, you’ll want to understand the root of the issue. Sewer pipes under concrete slabs can deteriorate over time due to various factors, such as the corrosion, cracks or breaks from shifting soil, and even tree root intrusion. All of these problems can lead to sewage backup, foul odors, and potential health hazards.

Pro Tip: Richard Trethewey, This Old House plumbing and heating expert, recommends using a video camera inspection before tearing into any concrete. On a recent project, the team ran a high-quality camera with a bright light through the pipe to look for crushed sections and root intrusion at the joints. “These are really terrific to get inside that pipe,” Trethewey says. “It saves us a lot of guess work.” Sewer Pipe Diagnostic Inspection

The initial phase of repairing a sewer pipe under a concrete slab involves a thorough inspection. Video Inspection

A video inspection is the most effective way to examine the interior of the sewer pipe. Here’s how it’s typically conducted:

Insert a snake attached with a high-quality video camera and a bright light into the sewer pipe—these are expensive but can be purchased at major home improvement stores or online retailers.

Slowly feed the camera through the pipe, carefully observing the interior condition.

Look for signs of damage such as cracks, tree root intrusion, or pipe misalignment.

Record the inspection for further analysis and to pinpoint the exact location of issues.

In the video, Richard Trethewey and his team use this method to identify root intrusion at several pipe joints, demonstrating the effectiveness of video inspection in diagnosing sewer pipe problems. Assessing the Damage

Once the video inspection is complete, professionals can determine the best course of action. They consider factors such as: The extent of root intrusion The overall condition of the pipe

The location and severity of any cracks or breaks

The potential for future issues based on the current state of the pipe

This assessment helps in deciding whether a full pipe replacement is necessary or if a less invasive repair method, like pipe lining, would suffice. Preparing for Sewer Repair

Before the actual repair process begins, some preparatory steps are necessary for a smooth and effective job. Clearing the Pipe

The first step in preparing the pipe for repair is to clear it of any obstructions:

Use a specialized drain snake with a cutter head attachment.

Richard Trethewey emphasizes the importance of working quickly once the epoxy is mixed, as it begins to set within 45 minutes. The Sewer Pipe Lining Process

The pipe lining technique demonstrated in the video offers a non-invasive solution to sewer pipe repair. This method creates a new pipe within the existing one, effectively sealing off any damage without the need for extensive excavation.

Pro Tip: Richard Trethewey, This Old House plumbing and heating expert, explains the technology: “The technology, called cured-in-place pipe (CIPP), basically involves inflating a resin-coated fabric balloon inside a pipe. After the resin hardens, the old pipe is left with a smooth new lining.” He notes that some CIPP systems use a polyester resin that doesn’t adhere to the inside of the pipe, which means the ends of any connecting pipes must be carefully sealed. Other systems use an epoxy resin that sticks to the wall of the old pipe, but the pipe has to be mechanically cleaned first to get good adhesion. Preparing the Liner

The first step in the process involves preparing the polyester liner: Lay out the polyester sock-like liner.

Use a traffic cone as a funnel to pour the mixed epoxy into the liner.

Feed the epoxy-filled liner through steel rollers to evenly distribute the resin. Installing the Liner

Before the liner is installed, a two-part epoxy must be carefully prepared. One component is a resin, the other a hardener. Once mixed, the epoxy is poured into the liner, and rollers are used to spread it evenly into the fabric. Timing is critical—as the installer on the This Old House project explained, “Once you mix them, how much time we got, it’s gonna be 45 minutes before it cooks off. We’re gonna get moving.” Here is how Richard set the liner:

Attach the prepared liner to a two-part air chamber.

Use compressed air to invert the liner as it’s fed into the existing pipe.

The inversion process turns the liner inside out, pressing the epoxy-coated side against the old pipe’s interior.
