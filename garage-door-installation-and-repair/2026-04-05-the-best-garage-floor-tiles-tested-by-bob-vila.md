While material is a vital factor to consider when shopping for the right garage floor tile, other attributes are important as well, including size, durability, installation, and even appearance. Ahead, learn more about these and other crucial characteristics of garage floor covering. Type

Garage floor tiles come in three different types. Understanding the advantages and disadvantages of each is crucial to selecting the right one for the project. Rigid Plastic

These hard floor tiles consist of PVC flooring plastic that’s strong enough to withstand the weight of a vehicle while resisting oil, grease, and other liquids and substances frequently found in a garage. Unlike softer materials, hard floor tiles can also endure the intense pressure created by a jack stand or floor jack holding up the weight of a vehicle. This makes them ideal for home mechanics. Rigid plastic garage floor tiles are the most expensive of the three options. Flexible

Flexible garage tiles consist of high-density foam or rubber. These floor tiles create a cushioned work surface that’s easier on the foot and knees than hard concrete or a rigid floor tile. This makes them a good option for garages that function as workshops. While they are durable enough to support the weight of a vehicle, they may not last as long as rigid materials. Although foam offers the best cushioning, it’s more likely to tear or rip under the weight of a metal jack or the friction of a wheeled tool rolling across it. Rubber, which is typically made from recycled tires, may not offer as much shock absorption, but it’s much more durable. It’s also significantly more expensive than foam. Peel and Stick

Flexible and rigid garage floor tiles both use an interlocking design that connects the tiles and holds them in place on the floor. Peel-and-stick tiles have an adhesive backing that attaches directly to the garage floor. Since this garage floor tile style does not have interlocking edges, it creates a cleaner look than flexible or rigid garage tiles. But these tiles consist of thin polyvinyl, so they don’t provide much shock absorption. They can also be tricky to install as each tile needs to be properly squared to the room to ensure all the tiles line up, and on a garage floor this can be difficult. Size

Garage tiles generally come in either 12-by-12-inch squares or 24-by-24-inch squares. While the larger tiles may install more quickly than smaller ones, they can be less forgiving when squaring the new flooring to the room, especially for stick-on types that are difficult if not impossible to readjust once in place. The size of the tile can also create a different look depending on the style. Smaller squares that use a checkerboard pattern of alternating colors can create a busier look than larger squares. Larger tiles have fewer visible seams, creating a more streamlined look than smaller tiles. Durability and Aesthetics

Since a garage floor must endure 2-ton vehicles, spills from various chemicals and cleaners, and the weight of heavy power tools, not to mention extreme temperatures (the garage is not typically climate controlled), the tiles must be durable. As such, a garage floor tile should be made of foam, recycled rubber, hard PVC plastic, or vinyl.

Just because a garage is a more utilitarian part of the house doesn’t mean it can’t have a little style. Floor tiles come in colors ranging from gray and black to bright orange and red to suit different tastes. Tiles also come in a variety of tread patterns, including coin and diamond patterns. If you’re wondering how to install floor tiles or how tiles compare to other garage flooring options, read on for this and other information in the answers to some commonly asked questions.

Q. Are garage floor tiles better than epoxy?

Both have their pros and cons. While garage floor coatings such as epoxy create a seamless hard shell that can last for decades, epoxy can be very tricky to install. Interlocking floor tiles are much easier to install and, like epoxy, offer a wide range of colors and textures; however, they don’t last as long as epoxy nor do they offer a seamless finished look. Q. How do I lay garage floor tiles?

To lay garage floor tiles, start in one of the front corners of the garage and work out from there, making sure the loops of the tile face toward the inner part of the garage. Run a row of tile along one wall and a row along the front of the garage door. This will ensure the rest of the tiles will be square to the room. Next, fill the remainder of the floor with tiles. Trim the tiles to fit against obstacles or the opposing wall. Q. How do I clean garage floor tiles?

Mix hot water with a household floor cleaner. Sweep the floor, then mop it, changing out the soapy fluid frequently for best results. Do not apply too much water or it may leak below the tile to the cement floor below.

Q. Can I put ceramic tile on a garage floor?

We do not recommend using ceramic tile or porcelain garage floor tiles​ as floor covering for garage floors. While ceramic tile may be suitable for a kitchen or a bathroom floor, it simply isn’t strong enough to endure the weight of a vehicle or the impact caused by heavy tools falling on it.

Q. How thick should garage floor tiles be?

A good polypropylene garage floor tile will be ⅝-inch thick (⅜-inch thick for rubber). This thickness gives the tile enough weight to sit flush to the floor and stay in place when working or driving over it. The thickness also ensures it can bear the weight of a vehicle without warping or cracking. Q. Can mold grow under garage tiles?

Garage floor tiles are designed to allow air to flow under the tiles, helping to dry any moisture that gets between them and the concrete floor, hence preventing mold growth.
