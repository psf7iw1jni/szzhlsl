Tool stats: 6 pounds, 18-volt lithium-ion battery with 45-minute charger; holds 110 nails ⅝ to 2⅛ inches long; drives 600 2-inch nails per charge, two to three per second. Includes nose-mounted LED, adjustable drive depth, single or sequential nailing, two-year tool warranty.

The Senco Fusion’s motor-driven design is a key differentiator in the cordless nailer category. Unlike combustion-type portable nailers that require fuel cells in addition to a battery, this tool’s flywheel mechanism fires nails instantly with no ramp-up time—a significant advantage for trim carpenters who need the same rapid response they get from pneumatic guns. As article author Sal Vaglica noted, these battery-powered nailers eliminate both the compressor and the restrictive air hose, giving pros full freedom of movement on the job site. Paslode 901000

My two cents: “The nailer is precise and well balanced.”

The Paslode uses a gas fuel cell and combustion-driven piston system. As described in This Old House Magazine, inserting the fuel cell, snapping on a rechargeable battery, and loading a strip of collated nails is all it takes to get started. The 6-volt battery operates a spark plug and a tiny fan, igniting the fuel-air mix to drive the piston against the nailhead at lightning speed—no compressor or hose required. Dewalt DC608K

Photo by Don Penny/Time Inc. Digital Studio Price: About $280

Tool stats: 7½ pounds, 18-volt lithium-ion battery with 60-minute charger; holds 110 nails, ⅝ to 2 inches long; drives 800 2-inch nails per charge, four to five per second. Includes adjustable drive depth, single or sequential nailing, three-year tool warranty.

Unlike other cordless brad nailers in this test that rely on disposable gas fuel cells to fire the piston, the DeWalt DC608K uses a motor-driven piston mechanism. That means no recurring fuel-cell costs and no waiting for a fresh cell when one runs out mid-project—a key advantage for DIYers tackling longer jobs like cabinet assembly.
